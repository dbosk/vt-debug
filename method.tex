\section{Method}
\label{method}

% TODO(#6) Anonymise the course (and institution) before submission; keep the
% concrete details for now.
We will study the students of prgi26, the autumn 2026 instance of the
introductory programming course DD1317 at KTH Royal Institute of Technology.
The course teaches Python to first-year students and is organised around a
series of laboratory assignments---from a first \enquote{Hello World} lab,
through functions and variables, input and error handling, repetitions and
lists, classes and objects, containers, and file handling---followed by an
individual project.
The course design of prgi26 will be based on the findings of the companion
paper \autocite{VTProgMisconceptions}: the teaching material is structured
around the patterns of variation derived there.
% XXX(#7) Update this paragraph when prgi26 is finalised in Canvas; the design is
% based on prgi25 with the variation-theory patterns from the companion paper.
This matters for the present study in two ways: the students will have
\emph{experienced} the patterns of variation as learners, and by the
generative-learning effect (\cref{debugging-as-learning}) we expect some of
them to start generating such patterns for themselves.

Not all students use the course material, however: some study on their own,
from other sources.
These students form a natural comparison group \emph{within} the cohort: they
do only what is mandatory for assessment---the labs, the exam and the
project---without experiencing the material's patterns of variation.
We therefore also record which students use the course material, through the
learning platform's activity data complemented by self-report in a
questionnaire.
We also have the students who know programming from before; we identify
them with a diagnostic quiz at the start of the course.

Let us remind ourselves about the questions that we have set out to
answer, and how we will answer them; the details follow in the coming
sections.

\rqpatterns*
Answered descriptively: we record the students' debugging episodes with
learnlog (\cref{learnlog-data}) and code them in variation-theoretic terms
(\cref{analysis}); this yields a catalogue of which patterns appear, in
what order, and how often.
\Cref{h:patterns} additionally predicts:
\hpatterns*
As stated this is a universal claim, which a single counterexample would
falsify; since the coding is necessarily noisy, we operationalise it
statistically: pattern completeness should separate successful from
unsuccessful episodes.

\rqdeep*
Answered by testing \cref{h:deep}:
\hdeep*
A questionnaire (\cref{approach-measure}) provides the measure of the
students' approach to learning, which we correlate with the coded
patterns; a think-aloud subsample (\cref{think-aloud}) validates both
the coding and the classification.

\rqsuccess*
Answered by testing \cref{h:success}:
\hsuccess*
The debugging outcomes---whether the bug was fixed, and in how many
edit--run cycles---derive from the recorded episodes (\cref{analysis}) and
provide the success measures; a diagnostic quiz (\cref{prior-knowledge})
provides the control for prior experience.

\rqexperts*
Answered descriptively---it has no hypothesis to test: we code the
teachers' and assistants' episodes identically (\cref{teacher-baseline})
and compare the two groups' patterns.

\rqgenerative*
Answered by testing \cref{h:generative}:
\hgenerative*
The material-use measure (\cref{material-use}) identifies the groups;
approach and prior experience are controlled for; since the groups are
self-selected, the answer is correlational.

The methodological questions are answered by comparing independent
measurements of the same thing.
\mqcoding*
Answered within the think-aloud subsample (\cref{think-aloud}): we code
the patterns from the logs and compare them with the patterns in the
recorded reasoning; agreement is what licenses coding the full cohort
from the logs alone (\cref{analysis}).
\mqagreement*
Answered by comparing the three approach classifications---the
questionnaire scores (\cref{approach-measure,end-measure}), the
interview-based classification (\cref{think-aloud}) and the coded
open-text answers of the quiz (\cref{prior-knowledge})---and reporting
the agreement between them.
Note, finally, that the overarching hypothesis---that debugging skill and
the deep approach to learning are the same underlying ability---is not
itself directly tested: \cref{h:deep,h:success} are consistent with the
two being identical, but also with two distinct abilities sharing a cause.
Answering the questions thus supports the overarching hypothesis by
inference to the best explanation, not by falsification.
The data collection follows in \cref{data-collection}, the analysis in
\cref{analysis}, the pilot of the coding in \cref{method-pilot}, and the
ethical considerations in \cref{ethics}.

\subsection{Data collection}
\label{data-collection}

We describe the data collection in the chronological order of the course:
what we collect at the start of the course
(\cref{prior-knowledge,approach-measure}), what we collect throughout it
(\cref{learnlog-data,think-aloud,material-use,teacher-baseline}), and what
we collect at the end (\cref{end-measure}).

\subsubsection{Prior programming knowledge}
\label{prior-knowledge}

Some students know programming from before the course.
They matter twice: prior experience is a confound for \cref{h:success}, and
the experienced students form yet another group within the cohort, whose
debugging may exhibit the patterns from the start.
We therefore administer a diagnostic at the start of the course: two
short quizzes, one mapping the students' background and one testing
prior programming knowledge.
(Both are set up in the learning platform by the literate program in
\cref{app:quiz}, which also prepares their results for analysis.)
The background quiz asks the following; between the questions we give
the reason for asking them.
% XXX(#8) Draft wording; to be piloted before the course starts.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Have you programmed before this course?
    Which programming languages have you used, and for roughly how long?
\end{enumerate}
Experienced students will debug faster regardless of their approach to
learning: they have already discerned many of the critical aspects the
novices still lack, so they recognise familiar bug classes outright,
with no new variation needed to discern them (compare the prerequisites
of the search in \cref{debugging}).
Prior experience is therefore the control variable for \cref{h:success}.
Whether the student has programmed in Python we do not ask separately:
the languages they list already tell us, and the analysis derives it
when the answer is coded (\cref{app:quiz-analysis}).
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item If you have programmed before: how did you learn---in a
    formal course, self-taught, or both?
\end{enumerate}
The self-taught are self-directed learners by definition, which bears
directly on the deep approach (\cref{rq:deep}): they may already generate
their own variation.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item How confident are you that you could write, run and fix a
    small program on your own?
    (five-point scale)
\end{enumerate}
Less confident students may give up or persist differently, which
confounds the persistence-based success measures of
\cref{h:success}---the time and the number of cycles to fix.
We ask before the debugging exercise that follows, so that the exercise
cannot colour the answer.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Here is a small Python program, and what happened when
    it was run.
    The programmer expected it to print 30.
\begin{minted}{python}
price = input("Price? ")
total = price * 3
print(total)
\end{minted}
\begin{minted}{text}
Price? 10
101010
\end{minted}
    How would you go about finding out what is wrong?
    Describe your steps---you do not need to fix the program.
    (open text)
\end{enumerate}
The described steps can be coded for variation-thinking, and since
every student responds to the same exercise, the answers are directly
comparable.
Note that the exercise shows only the surprising behaviour and never
hints at the aspect behind it (that \mintinline{python}{input} returns
strings): we \textcquote[p.~89]{NCOL}{should not point out those
aspects for them but let the students discern them by
themselves}---generating that discernment is exactly what we want to
observe.
This gives a baseline measure of the debugging approach, independent of
both the logs and the questionnaire: a third angle on the connection
between approach and patterns (\cref{rq:deep}), and the third
classification whose agreement \cref{mq:agreement} asks about.

The knowledge test is its own quiz because it differs in kind from the
background items: its questions have right and wrong answers, so it can
be graded---and the students may well want to know how they did.
They therefore see their score afterwards, but neither the correct
answers nor which items they got right: the same questions must measure
the gain at the end of the course (\cref{end-measure}), so they have to
stay fresh.
The questions of the knowledge test are written against the course's
intended learning outcomes---one per outcome area, as many as fit
alongside the approach questionnaire (the R-SPQ-2F, which we introduce
in \cref{approach-measure}) and the background quiz.
Being written against the outcomes, they measure prior knowledge and,
administered again at the end (\cref{end-measure}), knowledge gain in
terms of exactly what the course intends to teach.
They are published in full (\cref{app:quiz}).
The quizzes are inspired by tests written by Emma Riese and Camilla
Björn.
The remaining alternative---an established concept inventory, which
would buy comparability with other studies---we discuss in
\cref{inventory-discussion}.

The questions are the following; between them we give what each
measures.
Their design follows \citeauthor{NCOL}'s discussion of assessment, as
the debugging exercise above already did:
\textcquote[p.~91]{NCOL}{the questions should not point out the
relevant aspects of the problem to be solved, as this is exactly what
the students are supposed to find out (discern)}---so each stem
presents a program or a situation, and leaves the critical aspect for
the student to discern.
\begin{enumerate}[series=knowledgequiz]
  \item What does the following program print?
\begin{minted}{python}
count = 0
total = 0
while total < 10:
    total = total + 4
    count = count + 1
print(count)
\end{minted}
    (choices: 2, 3, 4, or that the loop never ends)
\end{enumerate}
Control structures: predicting the output requires simulating the
loop---the body runs a third time, since 8 is still below 10---and each
wrong choice is one simulation error: a lap too few, a lap too many, or
misreading the condition as never ending.
\begin{enumerate}[resume*=knowledgequiz]
  \item What does the following program print?
\begin{minted}{python}
a = [1, 2, 3]
b = a
b.append(4)
print(a)
\end{minted}
    (choices: \mintinline{python}{[1, 2, 3]},
    \mintinline{python}{[1, 2, 3, 4]}, \mintinline{python}{[4]}, or a
    crash)
\end{enumerate}
Containers and references: two names for one list, so appending through
one name is visible through the other---the aspect our worked example
turns on (\cref{debugging}).
\begin{enumerate}[resume*=knowledgequiz]
  \item A program needs the user's year of birth.
    Which line is the best option?
\begin{minted}{python}
year = int(input())
year = int(input("Year of birth? "))
year = int(input("Enter data: "))
year = int(input("y: "))
\end{minted}
\end{enumerate}
Usable interfaces: the four candidates differ only in the prompt, so
what varies is exactly how much the user is told.
\begin{enumerate}[resume*=knowledgequiz]
  \item What is the best improvement of the program below?
\begin{minted}{python}
length = float(input("Length of the living room? "))
width = float(input("Width of the living room? "))
area = length * width
print("The living room:")
print(area, "square metres")

length = float(input("Length of the kitchen? "))
width = float(input("Width of the kitchen? "))
area = length * width
print("The kitchen:")
print(area, "square metres")

length = float(input("Length of the bedroom? "))
width = float(input("Width of the bedroom? "))
area = length * width
print("The bedroom:")
print(area, "square metres")
\end{minted}
    (choices: move the lines that repeat into a function and call it
    once per room; add a comment above each block; use different
    variable names in each block; nothing---the program works, so it
    should not be changed)
\end{enumerate}
Dividing programs into functions, posed through its most common
trigger: repeated code.
The stem no longer names the repetition---seeing it is the point---and
only one choice addresses it: the others decorate it with comments,
mistake the reused names for the problem, or deny that there is one.
\begin{enumerate}[resume*=knowledgequiz]
  \item A program should print every line of the file
    \texttt{notes.txt}.
    Which statement is true?
    (choices: the file must first be opened, then the program can read
    its lines; \mintinline{python}{print(notes.txt)} prints the file's
    lines directly; reading is only possible if the file lies in the
    same folder as Python itself; files can only be read once and are
    then deleted)
\end{enumerate}
Transferring data between file and program: the correct choice is the
opening step, and each distractor is a common confusion---that the
file's name alone gives access to its content, that access depends on
the interpreter's location, and that reading consumes the file.
\begin{enumerate}[resume*=knowledgequiz]
  \item The program below crashes when the user answers
    \enquote{a few}.
    Which statement is true?
\begin{minted}{python}
guests = int(input("How many guests are coming? "))
\end{minted}
    (choices: \mintinline{python}{int} raises a
    \mintinline{python}{ValueError}, which the program could catch
    with \mintinline{python}{try} and \mintinline{python}{except};
    \mintinline{python}{input} cannot read answers that contain a
    space; the crash happens because \mintinline{python}{guests} has
    no value yet; Python programs cannot recover from errors)
\end{enumerate}
Robust interactive programs: the correct choice locates the error at
the conversion and knows it can be caught; the distractors misplace it
at the input or at the assignment, or declare it beyond recovery.
\begin{enumerate}[resume*=knowledgequiz]
  \item A library program models books with a class
    \mintinline{python}{Book}.
    Which statement is true?
    (the statements: the class describes what every book has, and each
    actual book is an object of the class; each book needs a class of
    its own; the class is created from the objects, one class per
    object; the class stores the title and author of one particular
    book)
\end{enumerate}
Composite data types: the correct statement is the class--object
relation; each distractor collapses it in a different way---one class
per book, classes derived from objects, or the class as one book's
storage.
\begin{enumerate}[resume*=knowledgequiz]
  \item You are asked to review the following program.
    Which of the suggestions below would improve it?
    Check all that apply.
\begin{minted}{python}
def f(x):
    y = x * 24
    return y

hours = int(input("Days? "))
print(f(hours))
\end{minted}
    (the suggestions: renaming \mintinline{python}{f},
    \mintinline{python}{x} and \mintinline{python}{y}; naming the
    variable after what it holds; a docstring; inlining the function)
\end{enumerate}
Reviewing others' code: renaming, naming after content and documenting
all improve the program while inlining the function does not---and the
item is multi-answer, so partial insight does not score.

The remaining stems pass \citeauthor{NCOL}'s test as well: they present
programs and behaviours---a printout to predict, a crash to
explain---and the aspect to discern (the aliasing, the conversion, the
repetition) is never named in the stem.
Choices necessarily name aspects; there the student's task is to judge
the named claims against the code, which is the reviewing skill itself.

\subsubsection{Measuring the approach to learning: R-SPQ-2F}
\label{approach-measure}

We measure the students' approaches to learning with the revised two-factor
Study Process Questionnaire (R-SPQ-2F), which yields a deep-approach and a
surface-approach score from ten items each \autocite{RSPQ2F}.
At the start of the course the students cannot yet answer with respect to
this course---they must have started the course for that---so this
administration asks about their studies in general, to get a feel for their
general approach.
(We administer the questionnaire again at the end of the course; we
motivate that in \cref{end-measure}.)

\paragraph{The items at the start of the course}

The R-SPQ-2F comprises 20 items: for each of the deep and the surface
approach, five motive and five strategy items, giving 10 items per
approach score \autocite{RSPQ2F}.
Each item is answered on a five-point scale, from \enquote{this item is
never or only rarely true of me} to \enquote{this item is always or almost
always true of me}; the deep-approach score sums items 1, 2, 5, 6, 9, 10,
13, 14, 17 and 18, and the surface-approach score the remaining ten
\autocite[pp.~148--149]{RSPQ2F}.
At the start of the course we use the questionnaire as printed in the
appendix of \textcite{RSPQ2F}, with its original instructions, which ask
about the student's studying in general.
The items are the following.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item I find that at times studying gives me a feeling of deep personal
    satisfaction.
  \item I find that I have to do enough work on a topic so that I can form
    my own conclusions before I am satisfied.
  \item My aim is to pass the course while doing as little work as
    possible.
  \item I only study seriously what's given out in class or in the course
    outlines.
  \item I feel that virtually any topic can be highly interesting once I
    get into it.
  \item I find most new topics interesting and often spend extra time
    trying to obtain more information about them.
  \item I do not find my course very interesting so I keep my work to the
    minimum.
  \item I learn some things by rote, going over and over them until I know
    them by heart even if I do not understand them.
  \item I find that studying academic topics can at times be as exciting
    as a good novel or movie.
  \item I test myself on important topics until I understand them
    completely.
  \item I find I can get by in most assessments by memorising key sections
    rather than trying to understand them.
  \item I generally restrict my study to what is specifically set as I
    think it is unnecessary to do anything extra.
  \item I work hard at my studies because I find the material interesting.
  \item I spend a lot of my free time finding out more about interesting
    topics which have been discussed in different classes.
  \item I find it is not helpful to study topics in depth.
    It confuses and wastes time, when all you need is a passing
    acquaintance with topics.
  \item I believe that lecturers shouldn't expect students to spend
    significant amounts of time studying material everyone knows won't be
    examined.
  \item I come to most classes with questions in mind that I want
    answering.
  \item I make a point of looking at most of the suggested readings that
    go with the lectures.
  \item I see no point in learning material which is not likely to be in
    the examination.
  \item I find the best way to pass examinations is to try to remember
    answers to likely questions.
\end{enumerate}
We keep the items verbatim, even where the wording could be adapted to
our setting---item 14's \enquote{free time} might read more naturally as
out-of-class time---since rewording a validated instrument would put its
validation, and the comparability with other studies, at risk.

\paragraph{Criticisms of the instrument}

Reading the items, one may object that many of the deep items express
interest in the topics---finding them exciting, spending free time on
them---rather than a way of going about learning them: a student can
surely take a deep approach to a topic they do not particularly care
for.
The objection points at the instrument's construction: each approach
score deliberately sums five \emph{motive} items and five \emph{strategy}
items, where the deep motive is intrinsic interest and the deep strategy
is to maximise meaning \autocite{RSPQ2F}.
The deep-approach score thus mixes wanting to learn with how one goes
about it.
For our purposes the strategy side is the one that matters---creating
patterns of variation is a way of going about learning, not a level of
interest---so we will also score the deep-strategy subscale separately
(items 2, 6, 10, 14 and 18 \autocite[p.~149]{RSPQ2F}) as a robustness
check; the analysis program in \cref{app:episodes} computes both.
Item-level factor analyses, however, advise care with that substructure:
\textcite{RSPQ2FReexamined} find the R-SPQ-2F
\textcquote{RSPQ2FReexamined}{best described by a parsimonious
first-order two-factor model}---deep and surface, with no separate
motive and strategy factors.
The instrument is also sensitive to context: comparing validation
studies across cultures, \textcite{RSPQ2FCrossCultural} conclude that
\textcquote{RSPQ2FCrossCultural}{cross-cultural sensitivity is an
important point to be borne in mind when using the R-SPQ-2F}; our
students answer the original English items but are mostly not native
speakers of English, so we will check the two-factor structure in our
own data before interpreting the scores.
The last safeguard is that the questionnaire is not our only approach
measure: the think-aloud accounts classify the approaches independently,
as we describe next.

\paragraph{Classifying the students compatibly with the original studies}

Some care is needed for our classification of students to be compatible with
the studies our theory rests on.
In the original studies the approaches were identified phenomenographically
and per task:
\textcquote[p.~142]{NCOL}{\textins{t}he distinction was based on the
learners' own accounts of how they went about learning (i.e., how they
experienced their own attempts to learn)}---the students were interviewed
about how they set about reading a particular text
\autocite{MartonSaljo1976I}.
The R-SPQ-2F is instead a self-report questionnaire; it does not classify an
actual learning episode but the student's reported way of studying.
We bridge this gap in three ways.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item In the end-of-course administration (\cref{end-measure}) we
    have the students answer the R-SPQ-2F with respect to \emph{this
    course}, in line with the instrument's intended use by teachers
    evaluating the approaches of their own students \autocite{RSPQ2F} and
    with the task-dependence of the approach \autocite{MartonSaljo1976II}.
    The resulting scores are comparable with the questionnaire-based
    literature.
  \item In the think-aloud subsample (\cref{think-aloud}) we additionally
    classify the students the way \textcite{MartonSaljo1976I} did: we ask them to recount how they
    went about the lab and the debugging, and classify the accounts as deep
    or surface.
    This classification is the one compatible with \citeauthor{NCOL}'s
    variation-theoretic reinterpretation, and we report the agreement between
    it and the questionnaire classification (\cref{mq:agreement}).
  \item We guard against circularity.
    \Citeauthor{NCOL}'s reinterpretation \emph{defines} the deep approach as
    creating the necessary patterns of variation
    \autocite[p.~145]{NCOL}; if we were to classify the students' approaches
    from the same edit--run logs that we code for patterns of variation,
    \cref{h:deep} would hold by construction.
    The approach classification therefore uses only the questionnaire and the
    interview accounts, never the logs.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Recording the students' programming: learnlog}
\label{learnlog-data}

We record the students' programming with the \texttt{learnlog} tool
\autocite{learnlog}.
The students run \texttt{learnlog init} once in their course directory (one
directory for the whole course, not one per assignment or project); the
tool then installs itself into the directory's virtual environment and
activates on every Python run, so that every program run is recorded
transparently---even runs that fail before the program starts, such as
syntax errors: source code changes, command-line arguments, standard
input/output/error, and unhandled exceptions are all stored.
The data is stored in a hidden local Git repository, which gives a complete
timeline of the students' edit--run cycles.
The students push their logs to per-student Git repositories (or export them
as bundle files and submit them through the course platform), and tag the
events per lab so that episodes can be attributed to assignments.
Since we encourage the students to run everything in this course-wide
directory, we hope to capture not only the lab work but also some of what
they run while studying---for instance when they follow a tutorial or try
out an example from the material.
When studying, they are more likely to aim for full understanding rather
than only fixing the problem at hand, so we expect to see more complete
patterns in the debugging they do while studying.

\begin{frame}<presentation>[fragile]
  \begin{block}{learnlog\only<presentation>{~\autocite{learnlog}}}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \texttt{learnlog init} once per course directory.
      \item Records every run: source diff, arguments, I/O, exceptions.
      \item Hidden local Git repo \(\to\) complete edit--run timeline.
      \item Students push logs or submit bundles; events tagged per lab.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

For the debugger, an edit--run cycle is exactly one experiment: a variation of
the program followed by an observation of its behaviour.
The sequence of edit--run cycles is therefore precisely the data in which
patterns of variation---what was varied, what was kept invariant, and in
what order---should be visible, if our hypotheses hold.
Recall the phenomenographic stance
(\cref{background}): acts express understanding, so the recorded acts are the
appropriate data for a second-order analysis.

\subsubsection{Think-aloud sessions with a subsample}
\label{think-aloud}

With a smaller subsample of student volunteers we also hold think-aloud
debugging sessions during the course, of the kind piloted in \cref{pilot}:
the student debugs while speaking their thoughts aloud, and we record the
session with learnlog (\cref{learnlog-data}) together with the audio and
video (over Zoom).
We hold the sessions at various points during the course, to capture the
students at different points of their learning.
The subsample serves two purposes.
First, the logs record acts but not the reasoning between them, so the
think-aloud sessions let us validate that the patterns coded from the logs
(\cref{analysis}) correspond to the patterns in the students'
reasoning---this answers \cref{mq:coding}, a methodological contribution
in its own right.
Second, the sessions give us the students' task-specific accounts of how
they went about the debugging, which we need to classify their approaches
the way the original studies did (\cref{approach-measure}).

\subsubsection{Course material use}
\label{material-use}

We need to know who uses the course material to be able to answer
\cref{rq:generative}: the groups compared there are defined by whether the
student has experienced the material's patterns of variation.
To identify these groups we measure each student's use
of the course material: the learning platform (Canvas) records which pages,
modules and embedded activities each student has worked through, and we
complement this with a self-report item alongside the R-SPQ-2F asking how the
student mainly studies (from the course material, from other sources, or a
mix).
The interactive material assignments (FeedbackFruits) give us detailed data
about the students' interactions with the material as well.

\subsubsection{The teachers' debugging: an expert baseline}
\label{teacher-baseline}

\Cref{rq:experts} asks about expert debuggers.
We cannot sample expert debuggers in general, so we use the course's teachers
and teaching assistants as our approximation of experts, and collect the same
kind of data from them as from the students.
They run learnlog while preparing course material and during live-coding
sessions (learnlog is designed for recording and replaying such sessions
\autocite{learnlog}), and we complement this with think-aloud debugging
sessions of the kind piloted in \cref{pilot}.
The approximation is imperfect---the teaching assistants in particular vary
in experience---so we record their programming experience alongside, which
also lets us relate pattern completeness to expertise within the group.
Their episodes are coded identically to the students'
(\cref{episode-coding}), giving an expert baseline: which patterns experts
generate, in what order, and how complete their episodes are.

The teachers are not the only expert baseline available.
The debugging literature itself codifies how experts debug:
\textcite{WhyProgramsFail} teaches debugging as the scientific
method---creating and verifying hypotheses about the failure through
experiments, conducted systematically---which is precisely the kind of
self-generated variation our coding looks for
(\cref{debugging-as-learning}).
Our main interest in this comparison is to verify that what the book
prescribes is essentially variation theory, without itself knowing that:
the prescribed hypothesis--experiment cycle should show up in our coding
as the contrast and generalisation patterns.

A further expert of a different kind is an AI coding agent (Claude
Opus).
Since learnlog records every Python run in the virtual environment it is
initialised in (\cref{learnlog-data}), initialising it in the
environment where the agent works records all the agent's runs, and the
agent's debugging episodes can then be coded identically to the humans'.
That gives us a third point of comparison: which patterns of variation,
if any, a state-of-the-art coding agent generates when it debugs.

\subsubsection{End-of-course measurements}
\label{end-measure}

At the end of the course we administer both start-of-course instruments
again: the knowledge test and the R-SPQ-2F.
(The end-of-course knowledge quiz is pushed to the learning platform by
the same program as the start-of-course one, \cref{app:quiz}.)

We need the knowledge test (\cref{prior-knowledge}) again because at
the start it measures what the students brought with them; administered
again at the end, the difference measures what they learned during the
course.
That gives us a learning-outcome measure that is independent of both the
grades and the debugging-success measures (\cref{analysis}), so we can
relate the generated patterns of variation not only to debugging success
but also to how much the student learned.
The end-of-course quiz adds a single item of its own, asking whether the
student prepared for it: preparation would confound the gain
measure, so we need to know (\cref{app:quiz}).

We need the R-SPQ-2F (\cref{approach-measure}) again because the approach
to learning is not a fixed trait---students adapt it to their conception of the task
\autocite{MartonSaljo1976II}---so the start-of-course scores measure the
students' general approach, not their approach in \emph{this} course.
At the end of the course we use the same 20 items; only the preliminary
instructions differ, asking the students to answer in connection with this
course rather than their studying in general.
This is exactly the use the instrument was revised for:
\textcquote[p.~146]{RSPQ2F}{it is useful to indicate in the preliminary
instructions (see Appendix) that you want your students to reply in
connection with the particular course, module, or programme in question,
rather than to studying generally}.
The two administrations thus differ only in the instructions, so the start
and end scores remain comparable while measuring the general and the
course-specific approach, respectively; the end-of-course scores are the
ones we treat as measurements of the approach in the context of this
course rather than as a stable property of the student.

\subsection{Analysis}
\label{analysis}

We define a \emph{debugging episode} as the sequence of edit--run cycles from
a run that surprises the student (typically a failing run, an unhandled
exception, or wrong output) to the run where the behaviour is understood and
fixed.
The \texttt{learnlog analyse} command generates reports of the edit--run
cycles \autocite{learnlog}; on these we perform a qualitative,
variation-theoretic coding:\label{episode-coding}

\begin{frame}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item For each consecutive pair of runs in an episode: what did the student
    vary, and what did they keep invariant?
  \item Classify the steps into the patterns: \emph{contrast} (varying the
    suspected critical aspect against the surprising behaviour),
    \emph{generalisation} (varying non-critical aspects, keeping the suspected
    aspect invariant---the experiments), and \emph{fusion} (varying the
    aspects together, reconciling the new understanding with the old).
  \item Rate the episode's pattern completeness: which of the patterns are
    present, and whether the episode ends in a fix.
\end{enumerate}
For each episode we also record whether the bug was fixed, the number of
edit--run cycles, and the wall-clock time from the first failing run to
the first passing run: these constitute our measures of debugging success
for \cref{h:success}.
\end{frame}

Note that when programming, the generalisation and fusion experiments are
probably not made in the lab file itself: they are typically done in a REPL
or a throw-away file.
Since learnlog activates on every Python run in the course directory's
virtual environment---including REPL sessions and scratch files---these
runs are recorded too, and the coding must consider all recorded runs, not
only those of the lab solution.

One possibility is that a student does not generate the generalisation and
fusion patterns at all, but instead searches for an explanation online or
asks an AI assistant (\cref{debugging-as-learning}).
In the logs such episodes show up as a sudden fix without preceding
experiments, possibly with pasted-in code; we code these as externally
sourced.
This matters for \cref{h:patterns}: a successful episode without patterns
is not a counterexample if the variation was outsourced---the hypothesis
concerns the episodes where the student generated the explanation
themselves.

This coding answers \cref{rq:patterns} (which patterns appear).
For \cref{rq:deep,rq:success} we relate the coded episodes to the R-SPQ-2F
scores and the debugging outcomes: correlating deep-approach scores with
pattern counts and completeness (\cref{h:deep}), and both with debugging
success (\cref{h:success}).
These computations, and the data formats they assume, are implemented in
the literate program of \cref{app:episodes}.

The within-cohort comparison group (\cref{material-use}) lets us answer
\cref{rq:generative} already in this study: if experiencing patterns of
variation breeds generating them, as \cref{h:generative} predicts, then the
students who use the course material should generate more, and more complete,
patterns in their debugging than the students who study on their
own---controlling for approach (R-SPQ-2F) and prior programming experience.
The groups are self-selected, not randomised, so this comparison is
correlational; we discuss the resulting confounds in \cref{discussion}.

For \cref{rq:experts} we compare the students' coded episodes with the
teachers' expert baseline (\cref{teacher-baseline}): which patterns each group generates, in
what order, and with what completeness.
If the overarching hypothesis is right, the experts' episodes should look
like the deep-approach students' episodes, only more so---and the
differences between the two should point out precisely what debugging
instruction needs to provide.
The experts can also be expected to have discerned more aspects than the
students: they have more to vary, and their prerequisites for the search
are richer (\cref{debugging}).

\subsection{Pilot}
\label{method-pilot}

As reported in \cref{pilot}, we have piloted the variation-theoretic coding on
think-aloud recordings of experienced programmers debugging the
default-argument phenomenon of \cref{debugging}.
The patterns were identifiable in the recordings, and the successful episodes
contained all of them.
The pilot used screen and voice recordings; the main study instead uses
learnlog's edit--run data, which scales to a full course but does not capture
the students' reasoning between runs.
We therefore complement the logs with the think-aloud subsample
(\cref{think-aloud}), to validate that the patterns coded from the logs
correspond to the patterns in the students' reasoning.

\subsection{Ethics}
\label{ethics}

Participation in the study is opt-in and requires informed consent; the
students' grades are unaffected by participation, and the logs are collected
and analysed pseudonymously.
(The consent item opens the background quiz, \cref{app:quiz}, an
arrangement kept from the course's earlier diagnostic tests.)
The learnlog data contains the students' source code and program
interactions, but no more than what the students already submit with their
lab solutions.
