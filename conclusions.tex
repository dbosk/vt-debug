\section{Conclusions}
\label{conclusions}

We have argued, from variation theory, that debugging is self-directed
learning: a bug marks a critical aspect the programmer has not yet
discerned, and it is the bug itself that opens the dimension---by
contrasting the actual behaviour with what was expected---while fixing it
requires generating the remaining patterns of variation, contrast,
generalisation and fusion, for oneself.
Since Marton connects the deep approach to learning to exactly this
self-generated variation, we hypothesise that debugging skill and the deep
approach are the same underlying ability.

The worked example showed what this looks like in practice: a debugging
episode in which the patterns appear one by one, ending not merely in a fix
but in an explanation---and in a newly opened dimension, the
construction/execution time of default arguments.
A pilot with experienced programmers supports \cref{h:patterns} for
experts: they could not explain the unfamiliar phenomenon until they had
generated all the patterns.

To test the hypotheses on students, we have designed a study for prgi26:
five research questions, four hypotheses, and the data to answer
them---every edit--run cycle recorded with learnlog, the approaches to
learning measured with the R-SPQ-2F at the start and the end of the course,
a diagnostic quiz for prior knowledge, a think-aloud subsample for
validation, the teachers as an approximation of expert debuggers, and the
students who study on their own as the within-cohort probe of the
generative-learning corollary.
\Cref{rq:patterns,rq:experts} are answered descriptively,
\cref{rq:deep,rq:success,rq:generative} by attempting to falsify
\cref{h:deep,h:success,h:generative}; the overarching identity claim is
supported by inference to the best explanation.

If the hypotheses hold, debugging instruction becomes instruction in
self-directed variation: teach the students what to vary and what to hold
invariant, and---by generative learning---teaching the course content
through patterns of variation should improve their debugging even without
explicit debugging instruction.
The study runs in the autumn of 2026; the coded episodes, whichever way
they fall, will also give computing education its first catalogue of
novices' spontaneous patterns of variation in debugging.

\begin{frame}<presentation>
  \begin{idea}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Debugging \(=\) learning without a teacher.
      \item Deep approach \(=\) generating one's own patterns of variation.
      \item Hypothesis: they are the same skill.
      \item prgi26 + learnlog + R-SPQ-2F will tell.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{idea}
\end{frame}
